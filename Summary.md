Grammatical vs. pragmatic meaning

This passage explains the distinction between semantics and pragmatics in language interpretation. Stanley Fish argues that understanding a sentence involves more than just knowing the meanings of individual words. At the grammatical level, semantics studies the meanings of words and how they refer to things in the world. However, fully understanding an utterance also requires knowledge of the social and communicative context in which it is spoken, which is the focus of pragmatics.
The text further discusses how some linguists believe that grammar and pragmatics cannot be clearly separated because language use is shaped by social conventions. For example, questions like “Could you please pass the salt?” are understood as polite requests not only because of context but also because such forms have become conventionalized in English. While linguists such as Noam Chomsky focus mainly on grammar and linguistic competence, others emphasize the importance of communicative competence, a concept developed by Dell Hymes, which includes knowing how to use language appropriately in different social situations. The passage concludes that truly understanding a language requires more than knowing grammatical rules; it also involves understanding how language functions in real communication.

### Sentence vs. utterance

This passage explains the difference between sentences and utterances in spoken and written language. Many people assume that communication normally consists of complete grammatical sentences, but in everyday speech this is often not the case. As Carter and Cornbleet point out, people usually speak to achieve a communicative purpose rather than to produce perfectly formed sentences.

The conversation example in the passage demonstrates this idea. While some parts of the dialogue contain full grammatical sentences, other parts consist only of phrases or incomplete constructions. Despite not being complete sentences, these expressions are still understandable because their meaning is clear from the context of the conversation.

For this reason, pragmatics focuses on the concept of the utterance rather than the sentence. An utterance includes any meaningful unit of communication, whether or not it forms a complete grammatical sentence. Meaning is therefore shaped not only by grammar but also by the context in which language is used.

### Speech act theory

The next subchapter focuses on speech act theory and explores ideas proposed by  J. L. Austin and John Searle. According to Austin, people perform various acts when they are speaking or writing. Austin differentiates three types of acts that are performed when speaking: locutionary acts, illocutionary acts, and perlocutionary acts. Locutionary acts refer to the literal meaning and grammatical form of an utterance, while illocutionary acts refer to the speaker’s intentions while uttering the sentence. Moreover, perlocutionary acts refer to the force behind an utterance that has an effect on the listener. However, the author states that illocutionary force, the speaker’s intentions, is the primary focus of the speech act theory. 

Speech acts, as the author reports, can be divided into five categories proposed by John Searle: assertives/representatives, directives, commissives, declarations, and expressives. Assertives or representatives are state facts that can be either true or false, for example, “Columbus discovered America in 1492”. Directives intend to make someone do something, for example, “Stop shouting”. Commissives are utterances that commit speakers to future action, for instance, “I promise to call you later”. Declarations are utterances that change reality, such as “I now pronounce you husband and wife”. Expressives express speakers' attitudes, for instance, “ I’m sorry for being so late”. 

Moreover, this subchapter explains how speech acts can be explicit or implicit, direct or indirect, and literal or non-literal. Firstly, explicit acts use a performative verb, a verb that literally names the speech act, such as apologize, declare, and congratulate. Whereas implicit acts convey the same meaning without the use of performative verbs. For example, “I’m sorry” is not a performative verb; nevertheless, it communicates the same message as “I apologize”. Secondly, in direct speech acts, the intention clearly corresponds to the grammatical form of an utterance, for example, “That’s enough go away”. In contrast, indirect speech acts imply the intention in a more polite or subtle manner. Lastly, speech acts can be literal or non-literal. As the author notices, speakers often say things that they do not really  mean and use figures of speech that are non-literal. For example, the expression “I’ll explain why in a minute” does not mean that the speaker will explain something exactly in a minute, but that the speaker wants to postpone the explanation. 

Finally, this subchapter introduces felicity or appropriateness conditions, which have to be satisfied for a speech act to be successful. The author refers to Searle, who proposed four felicity conditions: propositional content, preparatory, sincerity, and essential. To demonstrate how they work, the passage uses an apology as an example. For an apology to be successful, it needs to fulfil the propositional content, which means it has to be conveyed in a form that is associated with this speech act, for example, marked by the performative verb “apologize”. Secondly, the preparatory condition requires a belief that harm was done before making an apology. The sincerity condition refers to the fact that the speaker has to be sincerely sorry for what was done. And lastly, the essential condition requires an acceptance of the apology by the listener. However, the author notes that different speech acts can satisfy the felicity conditions in different ways. For example, a representative satisfies the propositional condition by being a statement that can be verified as true or false. Nevertheless, all speech acts must satisfy each felicity condition to be successful.

### The Cooperative Principle

This part of the chapter explains H. Paul Grice’s Cooperative Principle, which argues that effective communication relies on cooperation between speakers and listeners. Grice identifies four conversational maxims, such as, Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. Their function is to regulate discourse. When speakers intentionally or unintentionally violate (flout) these maxims, listeners often infer additional meanings called conversational implicatures.

The maxim of Quantity concerns informational adequacy, while Quality requires sincerity and evidential support. Relation emphasizes relevance, and Manner prioritizes clarity and brevity. The chapter illustrates how violations of these maxims may generate indirect meanings, often shaped by social conventions such as politeness.
	
This subchapter also addresses critique of this model, particularly its subjectivity. In response, Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson’s Relevance Theory proposes that communication is governed not by multiple maxims but the pursuit of optimal relevance, balancing cognitive effort with communicative value.
	
Overall, the subchapter highlights that meaning in discourse emerges not only from the statements themselves but also from pragmatic inferences grounded in conversational expectations. 

### Politeness
 
 The chapter provides a comprehensive overview of politeness in language, primarily grounded in the influential framework proposed by Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson. Their model conceptualizes politeness as a system centered on the notion of face, defined as an individual’s public self-image. Communication is thus shaped by the need to maintain both one’s own face and that of others, particularly when performing face-threatening acts (FTAs), which risk undermining social harmony. The degree of politeness employed in mitigating FTAs depends on three key variables: power relations between interlocutors, social distance, and the perceived severity of the imposition.

Power relations and social distance are presented as fundamental determinants of linguistic behavior. Hierarchical differences (e.g., teacher–student relationships) influence the use of honorifics and forms of address, which serve to signal respect and social positioning. Conversely, equal-status relationships tend to favor informal address forms. Social distance further refines these interactions, distinguishing between intimates and non-intimates. Intimates, regardless of power differences, typically employ terms of endearment and engage in more personal discourse, while greater social distance necessitates increased politeness and formality. Unlike many other languages, English lacks a formal grammatical distinction (T/V distinction) in second-person pronouns, relying instead on lexical and pragmatic strategies to encode politeness.

The chapter also emphasizes the role of indirectness and tact in mitigating FTAs, drawing on Geoffrey Leech’s politeness principle, particularly the tact maxim, which seeks to minimize cost and maximize benefit to the hearer. Speech acts such as directives and commissives are evaluated along a cost–benefit scale, where more indirect formulations are generally perceived as more polite. Speakers employ a range of mitigation strategies, including politeness markers (e.g., please, thank you), modal constructions (e.g., could you, would you), and indirect or “off-record” expressions that rely on implication rather than explicitness.

Beyond tact, the chapter outlines additional politeness principles identified by Leech, such as the generosity, approbation, and modesty maxims. These explain communicative behaviors like expressing gratitude, softening refusals, responding to compliments, and balancing praise and criticism. However, the chapter also highlights critiques of Leech’s framework, particularly the concern—articulated by Brown and Levinson—that the proliferation of maxims risks making pragmatic theory overly flexible and analytically weak.

Overall, the chapter demonstrates that politeness is a complex, context-sensitive phenomenon shaped by social structure, interpersonal relationships, and pragmatic strategies, with speakers constantly negotiating between clarity, efficiency, and the maintenance of social harmony.

### Speaker variables
 
 This chapter extends the discussion of pragmatic principles by introducing speaker variables as key determinants of linguistic variation. Drawing on the work of James Milroy and Lesley Milroy, language use is conceptualized not only as context-dependent but also as inherently tied to speakers’ social identities. These variables—such as gender, age, social class, ethnicity, education, and social networks—form the foundation of Sociolinguistics, which examines systematic variation in language across social groups.

The chapter emphasizes that speaker variables are not discrete but overlapping and interdependent. For instance, social class is closely linked to education, while gender intersects with ideological and identity-based dimensions, including sexuality. As noted by Deborah Cameron, there is an ongoing theoretical tension between viewing language as reflecting identity versus constituting it. Thus, sociolinguistic generalizations—such as the claim that women use more polite forms—must be understood as simplifications of more complex social dynamics.

Gender also plays a role, as seen in his relatively less frequent use of discourse markers such as like, which are more common among female speakers.

The second case study, Teresa B., highlights the role of ethnicity, education, and social mobility. As an African-American professional using General American English, her speech reflects both linguistic adaptation and social positioning. The chapter discusses the contested status of African American Vernacular English, including debates over its origins and the sociopolitical implications of the 1996 Ebonics controversy. Teresa’s experience illustrates how linguistic choices are shaped by broader societal attitudes, including racialized perceptions of “articulateness.”

A key concept introduced is social networks, defined as the web of relationships individuals maintain. Dense, localized networks (as in Michael’s case) tend to reinforce non-standard or localized forms, while more diffuse and geographically mobile networks (as in Teresa’s case) encourage linguistic flexibility. This flexibility is further manifested in style shifting, the ability to adjust speech according to context. Drawing on Allan Bell’s model of audience design, the chapter explains how speakers modify their language primarily in response to addressees, but also to auditors, overhearers, and contextual factors such as setting and topic.

Ultimately, the chapter demonstrates that linguistic variation is a dynamic interplay between social structure and individual agency. Speakers continuously navigate multiple identities and communicative demands, often shifting toward more formal or prestigious forms in contexts requiring greater social sensitivity. This reinforces the broader sociolinguistic insight that language is both a marker and constructor of social identity.
